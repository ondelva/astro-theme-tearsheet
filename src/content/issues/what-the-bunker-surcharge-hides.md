Bunker adjustment factors have an air of physics about them. A formula, a published index, a lookup table by trade lane. The implication is that nobody chose these numbers. Somebody did, and last week I spent three evenings comparing the published schedules of five carriers to find out who and how.

The setup is simple. Each carrier publishes a BAF per container, adjusted monthly or quarterly, based on the average price of fuel in a reference period. The formula is a baseline price, a consumption factor, and a trade lane multiplier. Where carriers differ is in the baseline, the rounding rule, and the lag between fuel moving and the surcharge following it.

A forwarder in Antwerp asked me a plain question that started this: "If fuel is down 6% this quarter, why is my BAF down 2%?" The honest answer needs the cut.

## The three places where the margin lives

**The baseline.** One top-five carrier's baseline is $480 per tonne. Another's is $520. The surcharge only counts fuel above the baseline, so a lower baseline means the surcharge starts earlier and is larger at any given market price. At $620 per tonne, the first carrier charges for $140 of fuel and the second for $100. That is a 40% difference in the amount of fuel the shipper is paying for, with nothing in the bill to explain it.

**The rounding.** The carrier I will call Meridian Lines rounds its BAF to the nearest $5, which is neutral. A second, Kestrel Marine, rounds up to the next $10. On a lane with a calculated BAF of $212, the first charges $210 and the second $220. A $10 difference is small per box. On the 36,000 boxes Kestrel moves on that trade each month, it is $360,000.

**The lag.** Most carriers use a trailing average of fuel prices, with a lag of one to three months. When fuel falls, the lag works for the carrier. When fuel rises, it works for the shipper. Over a full cycle it should roughly cancel. It does not, because carriers that publish quarterly reset their baseline only at their own discretion, and in my sample of eight quarters, six resets happened when fuel was rising.

## What the Antwerp question looks like in numbers

The forwarder's carrier uses a two-month lag and a $10 round-up. Fuel fell by 6% in the reference quarter, but only the second month of the decline reached the formula before the cutoff. Of the 6%, 3.1% got in. The round-up then took back $8. His BAF fell by 2%. Nothing about this is illegal or even unusual. It is also not what "fuel is down 6%" means to a person reading the news.

## A way to check your own carrier

You need three items: the carrier's published BAF table for the last two periods, the fuel index they reference, and a calculator. Compute the BAF the formula gives. If the number you compute is more than $5 below the number invoiced, ask which rounding rule applies.

I did this for my five carriers. Two matched to the dollar. One was $4 high in the carrier's favour. Two were $10 and $14 high, and when I asked, one of them replied with a corrected table the next morning. The other did not reply, which I note without comment.

## Why it matters more now

In a market where spot rates are falling faster than contract rates, as they are this month, the surcharge is the one component that does not follow the market down quickly. It is the stickiest line on the invoice, and the one with the fewest people looking at it. I would rather you looked. A shipper in Lyon told me she now keeps a one-page sheet per carrier with the baseline, the rounding rule and the lag written down, and refreshes it each quarter. It took her an afternoon the first time. In the second quarter she found a $14 per box error on a Mediterranean lane and recovered $5,600 for the year. She says the sheet has the best return of any document in her office, and that nobody else wants to read it.
