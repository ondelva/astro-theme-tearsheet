September is when the contract questions arrive. By now most shippers have seen two months of falling spot rates and one set of renewal letters from their carriers, and the two do not agree. These are the four questions that came in most often, lightly edited and anonymised.

### Should I renew now or wait for the October spot dip?

You are asking whether spot rates will fall further. They might. I would not build a contract strategy around that guess, because the useful question is how far apart spot and contract sit today on your lane, and what it costs you to be wrong in either direction.

On Asia to North Europe this week, the median contract rate for a 12-month deal is about $2,900 against spot at roughly $3,450. A contract that is 16% lower than spot looks like a bargain until you remember that contracts carry a minimum quantity commitment and spot does not. If your volume is steady, take the contract. If your volume swings by more than 30% across the year, the minimum is a trap, and I would move only part of it.

Waiting for an October dip is a bet that has paid off in two of the last five years. In the three it did not, the shipper renewed in November at a rate higher than the one on offer in September. The average cost of waiting, across those five years, was about $90 per box. The average gain was about $210 in the two years it worked. That is close to even, and I suspect it is worth less than the peace of mind of a signed paper.

### Can I split my volume between two carriers?

Yes, and most mid-sized shippers should. A common split is 70/30, with the larger share to the carrier that has the better schedule reliability on your lane. The smaller share keeps a second relationship alive and gives you a price reference.

The catch is that carriers see your split. A carrier that receives 30% of your volume will treat you as a secondary customer in a tight week. I have heard this described, by a sales manager, as "the courtesy you pay for being promiscuous". That seems a little hard. I would say the cost is a slight drop in priority, and that you can limit it by making the minimum for each carrier something you will definitely meet.

### What happens if I miss my minimum quantity commitment?

It depends on the contract. The usual remedy is a shortfall payment, commonly $150 to $400 per missing container, or the loss of the contract rate on remaining volume. The shortfall is rarely enforced in the first year of a relationship and almost always enforced in the third.

A forwarder in Gothenburg told me that in 22 years she has seen a carrier waive a shortfall twice. In both cases, the shipper had a larger account elsewhere. I would not rely on goodwill. Read the clause on shortfall carefully, and if there is no cure period, ask for 30 days.

### Is an index-linked contract worth the hassle?

Index-linked contracts tie your rate to a published spot index, with a discount or premium. They are less common than they were, and they are good for shippers who can accept a rate that moves every week.

The honest advantage is that you stop arguing about rate cards. The honest disadvantage is that you start arguing about the index. In the contracts I have read, the reference index and the date it is read vary: some use Thursday's, some the average of the previous week. A one-day difference on a volatile week can be $120 per box. Ask which index, which day, and who corrects errors.

If you like your forwarder and your volumes are modest, a fixed rate with a clean shortfall clause is boring and nearly always sensible. Boring has a good record in freight.

One closing note from the mailbag. Several of you asked whether to trust a carrier's "final offer" letter. The pattern in my notes is that the second letter is lower than the first by about $60 per box roughly half the time, and that the phrase "final" has no measurable effect on this. Reply politely, ask for the date by which the offer lapses, and if it lapses on a Friday afternoon, reply on Monday.
