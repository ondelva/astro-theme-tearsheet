Dwell time is the number of days an import container sits inside a terminal between discharge and gate-out. It is published, in some form, by most large terminals, and it is almost always quoted as a median. I collected the figures for four ports in the last week of October and want to show why I think the median is the wrong place to look.

The median describes a typical box. The people who call me are not holding typical boxes.

<!-- cut -->

## The numbers

These are medians for import containers discharged in the week, as published by each terminal operator or port authority, rounded to one decimal:

- Rotterdam, 2.1 days. The 90th percentile was 4.8 days.
- Hamburg, 3.0 days. The 90th percentile was 7.2 days.
- Savannah, 3.9 days. The 90th percentile was 8.5 days.
- Long Beach, 5.4 days. The 90th percentile was 12.0 days.

Only Rotterdam and Hamburg publish the 90th percentile directly. For the other two I estimated it from the share of boxes still in the yard after seven days. Treat those two with caution.

## Why the tail matters

The ratio between the 90th percentile and the median is 2.3 for Rotterdam, 2.4 for Hamburg, 2.2 for Savannah and 2.2 for Long Beach. So the shape is similar everywhere. The consequence is not. At Long Beach, one box in ten waited more than twelve days. Free time before storage charges is commonly five to seven days. A tenth of importers there were paying storage on a tenth of boxes, in a week with nothing unusual going on.

What drives the tail is rarely the terminal. It is a customs hold, a missing release from the carrier, a consignee without a trucking slot, or a box on a chassis that nobody has collected.

## What I would ask for

If you contract with a haulier or a terminal, ask for the 90th percentile of your own boxes over the last quarter, not the published median of everyone's. Your own figure is almost always worse than the port's, because importers with simple cargo do not call analysts. A company I spoke to found that its own median was 4.6 days at a port that published 2.9. The difference was a recurring paperwork delay on one commodity code. It cost them about 6,000 euros a quarter in storage.

I will return to dwell in January, when the year-end peak has had time to show up in the data.
