Three of nine Asia to North Europe loops skipped Port Klang in September. None of them announced a blank sailing. Taken together, the omitted calls pulled about 41,000 TEU of weekly capacity out of the lane, and nobody had to call it a cancellation.

I found this by doing the dullest possible thing: opening the schedule feeds on a Tuesday and checking, service by service, whether each loop's rotation still listed the port it listed last month. Three did not. On each, the Klang call was replaced by an extra day at sea, with an unchanged service code and an unchanged first and last port. A reader skimming the carrier website would see a weekly service from Busan to Rotterdam, as before.

The effect on prices was not what you would expect. Removing capacity should raise rates, and for two weeks it did. Then, on the fourth week, spot rates on Busan to Rotterdam fell from $3,450 to $3,140, a drop of 9%, while the contract rate index barely moved. Here is the explanation I believe.

<!-- cut -->

## Who blinked

Not every carrier cut capacity quietly. Of the nine loops, three skipped Klang, two added a call elsewhere, and four held rotations unchanged. The ones that held, I would call the confident, and the ones that skipped, the cautious. The distinction matters because the confident carriers are the ones that dropped their spot offers first.

The week of 21 September, the three skipping carriers raised their spot offers by about $120. Two of the four holding carriers cut theirs by $200. By 28 September, the cutters had moved the market. The skippers followed on 1 October, discounting by $310 to fill what was left of the weekly allocation. The result is a net fall, with the cautious carriers having lost money twice: once in the capacity they withdrew, and once in the discount they gave to refill it.

I have a number from a forwarder in Rotterdam which supports this. Of her 40 weekly Busan bookings, 31 went to one of the holding carriers last week, up from 24 in August. She did not choose them for their rotation. She chose them because they were $190 cheaper and her customer asked.

## Why contract rates did not move

Contracts are a different animal. A 12-month contract signed in March at $2,880 per box does not care about the October spot. What does move is the behaviour around the contract: a carrier with an unfilled sailing will take spot cargo ahead of a contract holder whose minimum quantity has already been met, since the contract holder will pay the same regardless.

I asked three forwarders whether they had seen this and all three said yes. One put it with some color: "Contract customers are the ones who get told the ship is full. The spot customers get the cheaper box and the earlier loading." That is not universal, and I would not build a strategy on it. It does explain why contract indexes move so little in a week where the spot lane is moving 9%.

## What the $3,140 contains

The all-in figure breaks down as ocean freight $2,380, bunker and low-sulphur surcharges $410, origin terminal handling $190, and forwarder margin and documents $160. The margin is thin because it is a competitive week. Two weeks ago it was $230.

The ocean freight line is the one that fell. In August it was $2,640. The surcharges did not move at all, which is a point I made about the bunker line two issues ago and will keep making. You can fight for a lower ocean rate; the surcharges do not respond to market pressure, only to the formula.

## What I would do this week

If you are on spot, book the holding carriers and ask for the following sailing too, because the lowest quotes are for a specific departure and the next one is $90 to $140 higher. If you hold a contract on this lane, ask your carrier whether the Klang omission affects your transit time, and request a written answer.

If you are a forwarder, the question to put to your carrier is whether the three skipping services will resume the Klang call. Two of them will, I think, by the end of October, which would put 27,000 TEU of capacity back and take another $150 out of the spot rate. I would hold off on committing to a fixed price beyond three weeks.

And if you are an analyst, a final small suggestion. The number to watch is not the spot rate, or even the contract rate. It is the gap between them, which this week is $260, down from $570 in June. When it closes entirely, someone has run out of room to discount. It would be a better indicator if it did not take a dull Tuesday to compute.
