title: "Reading a carrier's Q3 call in eleven minutes"
dek: 'Earnings calls are two hours of language built to say nothing precisely. Here is the eleven-minute version, with a stopwatch.'

A top-five carrier held its third-quarter call on Thursday. It ran 94 minutes. I have listened to enough of these to know that about eleven minutes contain information a freight buyer can use, and that those eleven minutes are not where the chief executive thinks they are.

The method is not clever. I skip the prepared remarks, which were written by a committee and read by a person who has heard them four times. I go to the question and answer section, and I read the transcript first, because voices lie better than text. Then I listen only to the answers where the analyst asked about a number and got a sentence instead.

Here is what I found, in the order it appears.

At minute 12, the chief financial officer was asked about contract renewals. She said that "volumes under contract for the coming year are in line with historical norms". That is a sentence with no number in it, and the analyst noticed. The follow-up asked for the percentage, and the answer was 61%, down from 68% a year earlier. That is a seven point drop in contract coverage, which means the carrier is leaving more cargo exposed to spot pricing, and is betting on a recovery.

At minute 31, the head of network planning said the company had "optimised" two services in the quarter. Translate this as: two loops lost a port call each. I checked the schedule feed and found Port Klang omitted on both, from the week of 8 September.

At minute 47, an analyst asked about idle capacity. The answer was that idle tonnage was "at structural minimum". The company's own fleet report, filed the same day, showed 2.1% of capacity idle, up from 0.8% in June. These two statements are about the same fleet in the same quarter. The difference depends on what "structural" means, and I have never heard a definition that survived a follow-up.

At minute 58, the first honest sentence: "We would expect to see rates under pressure through the fourth quarter." No qualifier, no hedging adverb. I listened to it three times. It is rare to hear an executive predict worse numbers without immediately balancing the sentence with a reason for hope. The chief executive added one, about demand in the new year, but it came eleven seconds later.

That is where the free part of this issue stops, because the next three moments are the ones you can act on.

At minute 71, a question about the Suez route produced the most useful thing in the call. The company is planning to keep 70% of its Asia to Europe capacity on the Cape of Good Hope route through the first half of next year. That is a planning assumption, not a prediction, but it is the number their network team is working from, and it tells you what their schedule will look like in January.

At minute 83, the line about capital expenditure. Two vessel orders were deferred by three months. The company called this "prudent". Deferral of a new build, in a market where average ship age is falling and spot rates are weak, is a sign of a carrier that expects to need less capacity next year than it said last year.

## What I would do with it

Three actions, none of them dramatic. If you are negotiating a contract with this carrier, the 61% coverage figure is leverage, because they want volume under contract and you have it. If you ship via the two omitted Port Klang services, plan for a change in transit times from October. If you are watching the fourth quarter for rate relief, take the minute 58 sentence at face value.

One more habit: I write down the questions the analysts asked and did not get answered. This call had four, including one about the share of volume on spot-indexed contracts, which got the answer "we do not break that out". A carrier that does not break out a number it certainly knows is telling you the number is unflattering. I file those four as open items and check them against next quarter's slides, where about half of them quietly appear as new disclosures.

The timing method is the point. I keep a stopwatch note of every minute where a number appears that was not in the slides. On this call there were nine such moments in 94 minutes, which is a typical density. Eleven minutes of listening, then, and the rest is for people who enjoy adjectives.
