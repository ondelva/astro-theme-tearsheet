On 2 June a forwarder I will call Kestrel Forwarding sent a customer a quote for one 40-foot container from Qingdao to Felixstowe: $3,280 all in, valid for seven days. On 19 July the customer paid an invoice for that box. The invoice said $3,911. Nothing improper happened in between. That is the interesting part.

This issue follows that quote through its seven stages and names the point at which each extra dollar entered. I have the documents, the dates, and the permission of the customer, who wants to remain nameless and a little less surprised in future. It is a long issue, because the story is long. Seven stages, two sailings, one ship that was not the ship anyone expected, and a spreadsheet that nobody updated on a Friday.

I will say now what I think the lesson is, so you can disagree with it as you read: a spot quote is not a price. It is the opening position in a negotiation that continues, silently, for six weeks after you accept it. Most shippers do not notice the negotiation because most of the movements are small and each has a plausible name.

## Stage one: the quote

The $3,280 was built from four components. The ocean freight was $2,450. Bunker and low sulphur surcharges added $410. Origin terminal handling at Qingdao was $190. The remaining $230 was the forwarder's margin and a documentation fee. Kestrel had a contract allocation with a carrier and priced off that, adding its markup. This is entirely normal.

What is less well known is that the ocean freight line was Kestrel's cost on the day it quoted, and not on the day it booked. The forwarder had a rate sheet dated 28 May. By 2 June, the carrier had issued a new sheet effective 1 June with the ocean rate for that lane at $2,520. Kestrel's quote still used the 28 May figure. Somebody had not refreshed a spreadsheet, and the cost of that was the forwarder's to absorb or recover.

Kestrel chose recovery. That will become important in stage five.

## Stage two: the booking

The customer accepted on day four of seven. The booking was confirmed for a sailing on 14 June, loaded at Qingdao Qianwan terminal. The confirmation listed the quote value, the carrier's service code, and a line that read "subject to carrier availability and surcharges applicable at time of shipment."

That sentence is boilerplate, and it is also the contract. "Applicable at time of shipment" means any surcharge the carrier announces between booking and loading is eligible to appear on the invoice. In this case, none appeared at this stage, but the clause waited.

The booking confirmation also gave a gate-in cutoff of Wednesday 12 June at 12:00. On 10 June the cutoff moved to Tuesday at 18:00. Nobody emailed the customer, because the change was posted to a portal. The customer's logistics coordinator saw it on Monday afternoon, partly by luck and partly because she keeps the portal open on a second monitor. She told me she has not missed a cutoff in four years and attributes it to the second monitor.

## Stage three: the first rollover

The 14 June sailing was full. A container booked on day four of seven, with a lower margin of priority than contract cargo, was rolled to the next sailing on 21 June. This is a common occurrence in a busy week. Carriers roll spot cargo first and keep their relationships with contract holders intact. The customer was told, correctly, that this was a risk of spot booking.

A rollover costs a week of the customer's time and, under some terms, nothing in money. In this case, the one-week delay moved the cargo into a different billing period for surcharges. The carrier's peak season surcharge, announced on 4 June, took effect for sailings from 17 June. The rolled container, loading on 21 June, was now inside it.

The peak season surcharge for this lane was $300 per container. Kestrel's booking was subject to surcharges applicable at time of shipment. So the container now carried $300 more than the one the customer originally booked, through no action of anyone present.

## Stage four: the ship

The 21 June sailing was operated by a vessel built in 2013 with a nameplate capacity of 13,800 TEU. It had replaced a newer ship on that service for that week. This had no direct cost for the customer, but it shows how the thing works: the same service code can carry cargo on very different vessels, with different speeds and different reliability.

The older ship had a longer transit by two days because it slow-steamed to save fuel. Arrival at Felixstowe was therefore scheduled for 17 July, and not 15 July as originally told. The customer's warehouse had slots reserved for 15 July. They kept them, then had to reschedule, and paid $85 to move the booking at the warehouse. This did not appear on any freight invoice, but it is real money and belongs to the quote.

## Stage five: the forwarder recovers

When the invoice was assembled, Kestrel needed to recover the $70 difference between its stale rate sheet and the current one. The method it chose is one I have seen at three forwarders: it added an amount to the bunker line, using the phrase "BAF adjustment per carrier notification." The customer saw a bunker line of $480, not $410, and nothing else about it looked wrong.

There was a real carrier notification. It said the BAF had risen by $12 that month. The forwarder added $70.

I asked Kestrel about this. A manager there said it was a "rounding of the reconciliation," which is a phrase I admire as one admires a well-made knife. When I pushed, she said the alternative would have been absorbing a loss on a loyal customer, and that on the next quote they gave the customer a lower margin to compensate. I have no way to check that. The next quote I saw did carry a lower margin, by $20.

## Stage six: the terminal

At Felixstowe, the container sat for two days longer than planned because the customer's warehouse slot had been moved. The terminal's free period was four days. The container used five. One day at $95 appeared on a separate demurrage note from the carrier, and this one the forwarder passed on without markup, with an apology.

Terminal handling at destination, quoted as "excluded" in the original quote, was $265. The customer knew about this and had budgeted for it. It is not part of the $3,911 and I list it only for completeness, because the total cost of the box to the customer was closer to $4,271 after the terminal and the warehouse fee.

A note on the clock, since the dates in this story matter more than the dollars. From quote to invoice took 47 days. The customer's own payment terms were 30 days from invoice, so the forwarder financed the carrier for about a month and a half before seeing a cent. Kestrel told me its cost of carrying a typical box in that period is $14 to $22. That is not nothing against a $230 margin, and it explains some of the behaviour in stage five without excusing it. A forwarder that is paid late and charged early has every reason to round in its own favour. Shippers who negotiate shorter payment terms, 14 days instead of 30, often find the "rounding" gets smaller. I cannot prove the link. Two forwarders I asked agreed that it exists, and a third said it was a coincidence, then offered me a 14-day deal.

## Stage seven: the invoice

Here is how the $3,280 became $3,911.

| Component                      | Quoted | Invoiced |
| ------------------------------ | ------ | -------- |
| Ocean freight                  | $2,450 | $2,520   |
| Bunker and low sulphur         | $410   | $480     |
| Origin handling                | $190   | $190     |
| Peak season surcharge          | $0     | $300     |
| Forwarder margin and documents | $230   | $230     |
| Demurrage, one day             | $0     | $95      |
| Documentation amendment        | $0     | $96      |
| Total                          | $3,280 | $3,911   |

The $96 documentation amendment is a charge for correcting the bill of lading after the rollover changed the vessel name. Carriers charge for this, and forwarders pass it on. It was the only line item that the customer queried, and it was the one that, in my reading, was most defensible.

Of the $631 increase, $70 is the stale rate sheet recovered through the bunker line. Another $70 is the ocean rate difference itself, which Kestrel absorbed in its own mind but invoiced anyway. $300 is the peak season surcharge, entirely a function of the rollover. $95 is demurrage, caused by the late arrival. $96 is the documentation fee. Add them in a different order and you get the same figure.

## What the customer could have done

I put this question to the customer's logistics coordinator. She had three observations.

First, she would have asked at booking for the quote to be "all-in including known surcharges for the sailing date and the following sailing". Forwarders will often agree to this when asked. She did not ask, because she had not seen a rollover cost money before.

Second, she would have objected to the BAF line. She noticed the $70 but did not challenge it, because the sum was small relative to the box and the relationship with Kestrel was good. She calls this "the tax on being polite", and estimates that she has paid it a dozen times in six years.

Third, she would have kept the original quote email and the invoice in the same folder. They had been in different folders, which is why it took her until July to see the whole gap.

## The version without a villain

It would be easy to read this as a story about a forwarder cutting corners. I do not think that is the right reading, and Kestrel would be an unfair target. Its margin on this box, after the adjustments, was about 7%, which is ordinary. It had a stale rate sheet because its rate team of two people maintains roughly 900 lane sheets, and the carrier issues revisions on a schedule that suits the carrier. The $70 recovery was clumsy, not clever.

The structural point is that every party in the chain is permitted to change its price within a window, and no party is required to tell the next one why. The carrier updates its sheet and tells the forwarder through a portal. The forwarder updates its invoice and tells the customer through a line item. The customer, who has the least information and the most exposure, is the only party who treats the original number as a commitment.

That asymmetry is the whole business model of spot freight, and I do not say this to be cynical. A spot market works because someone is willing to carry price risk. In a spot quote, the shipper carries it, in six small instalments, without being told.

## Three questions to put in an email

If you take nothing else from this issue, send these three questions to your forwarder before you accept a spot quote. They cost nothing and they change the conversation.

First: what surcharges are announced but not yet effective for this lane, and do they apply if my cargo rolls to the next sailing? The answer is usually yes, and the question makes the forwarder say so in writing.

Second: what is the date of the rate sheet this quote is based on? If the answer is more than four days old, the quote has probably aged.

Third: if my cargo is rolled, will you notify me within 24 hours, and who pays for the documentation amendment? This one earns its place because nobody has an answer prepared, which means the person you are talking to has to decide, in that moment, what is fair.

## What I take from it

The change from quote to invoice here was 19%, which is above the typical range I see but not outside it. Roughly one in six spot shipments I track ends up costing more than 10% above its quote. The causes are not exotic. A rollover is the most common. A late surcharge announcement is next. A forwarder recovering its own earlier error is third and the least discussed.

There is a mild irony in how the industry talks about this. Everyone on the shipper side asks for "price transparency" and everyone on the carrier side promises "full visibility". Neither word has any weight in the stage between a quote and an invoice, where the real movement happens. A shipper who wants a price that stays put has to buy a price that stays put, which means a contract, a fixed-rate booking with a guaranteed sailing, or a quote with a written clause that says what happens on a rollover.

None of these is free. A guaranteed sailing on that lane would have cost about $180 more on 2 June. The customer would have paid $3,460, and received a container loaded on 14 June, arriving on 15 July, with no peak season surcharge and no warehouse reschedule. In the end she paid $3,911 plus $85 plus $265 at the destination, $4,261 in all, against $3,725 on the guaranteed route. The guarantee would have come out $536 cheaper in total, about three times its price.

That is the part worth taking away, and I would not generalise it. Guarantees cost money in the weeks where nothing goes wrong. They save money in the weeks where something does. The decision is a bet on how often something goes wrong, and my tracking suggests it is about one in six in this market and this season. At those odds, a $180 guarantee against an expected loss of around $100 per box looks marginal, and the answer depends on how much you value not having to read invoices in July.
